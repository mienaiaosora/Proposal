# Quantification Plan: General Equilibrium with Exogenous Location Characteristics

## Objective

The first quantitative exercise should solve and calibrate a benchmark general equilibrium in which location characteristics, zoning capacities, and bilateral commuting costs are exogenous.

This benchmark should reproduce the observed metropolitan economy before introducing:

- endogenous traffic congestion;
- route choice;
- productivity agglomeration;
- endogenous residential congestion;
- municipal zoning choices;
- transportation investment; or
- coordinated-policy counterfactuals.

Building the model in layers makes it possible to distinguish calibration problems from problems caused by the policy game or the network equilibrium.

---

## 1. Benchmark assumptions

Set productivity and residential amenities equal to exogenous location fundamentals:

$$
A_j=\bar A_j,
\qquad
B_i=\bar B_i.
$$

Temporarily switch off agglomeration and endogenous amenity congestion:

$$
\zeta=a=b=0.
$$

Use a fixed matrix of bilateral generalized commuting costs, $\{\tau_{ij}\}$, rather than solving the edge-level network equilibrium. Thus, the first benchmark does not require the route-choice parameter $\theta$ or the network parameters $\rho$ and $\beta$.

Total metropolitan population $N$ is fixed, so this is a closed-city equilibrium.

### Exogenous objects

The exogenous inputs are

$$
\mathcal D
=
\left\{
N,\bar H_i^R,\bar H_j^F,\tau_{ij},
\bar A_j,\bar B_i,T_i,E_j
\right\},
$$

together with the parameters

$$
\alpha,\qquad \gamma,\qquad \epsilon.
$$

### Endogenous objects

The equilibrium determines

$$
\left\{
\pi_{ij},N_{ij},N_i,L_j,w_j,q_i^R,q_j^F,\Phi,\bar U
\right\}.
$$

---

## 2. Benchmark equilibrium system

### 2.1 Household residence–workplace choice

For residence $i$ and workplace $j$, deterministic indirect utility is

$$
V_{ij}
=
\frac{\bar B_iw_j}
{\tau_{ij}(q_i^R)^{1-\alpha}}.
$$

The probability of choosing $(i,j)$ is

$$
\pi_{ij}
=
\frac{T_iE_jV_{ij}^{\epsilon}}
{\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}T_mE_nV_{mn}^{\epsilon}},
$$

and the corresponding number of commuters is

$$
N_{ij}=N\pi_{ij}.
$$

Residential population and workplace employment are

$$
N_i=\sum_jN_{ij},
\qquad
L_j=\sum_iN_{ij}.
$$

The choice probabilities automatically satisfy

$$
\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}=1,
\qquad
\sum_iN_i=\sum_jL_j=N.
$$

### 2.2 Residential market clearing

Housing demand by an $(i,j)$ household is

$$
h_{ij}^{R*}
=
\frac{(1-\alpha)w_j}{q_i^R}.
$$

Residential market clearing implies

$$
\sum_jN_{ij}
\frac{(1-\alpha)w_j}{q_i^R}
=
\bar H_i^R,
$$

or, equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
q_i^R
=
\frac{(1-\alpha)\sum_jN_{ij}w_j}
{\bar H_i^R}
}.
$$

### 2.3 Labor-market clearing

With exogenous productivity, firm labor demand is

$$
L_j
=
\left(
\frac{\gamma\bar A_j}{w_j}
\right)^{1/(1-\gamma)}
\bar H_j^F.
$$

The corresponding equilibrium wage is

$$
\boxed{
w_j
=
\gamma\bar A_j
\left(
\frac{\bar H_j^F}{L_j}
\right)^{1-\gamma}
}.
$$

### 2.4 Commercial rents

The firm's first-order condition for commercial floor space gives

$$
q_j^F
=
(1-\gamma)\bar A_j
\left(
\frac{L_j}{\bar H_j^F}
\right)^\gamma.
$$

Constant returns also imply

$$
q_j^F\bar H_j^F
=
\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}w_jL_j.
$$

### 2.5 Welfare

Define

$$
\Phi
=
\sum_{i,j}
T_iE_j
\left[
\frac{\bar B_iw_j}
{\tau_{ij}(q_i^R)^{1-\alpha}}
\right]^\epsilon.
$$

Ex ante expected household utility is

$$
\bar U
=
\Gamma\left(1-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
\Phi^{1/\epsilon}.
$$

This closes the benchmark equilibrium.

---

## 3. Numerical solution

A damped fixed-point algorithm can solve the benchmark.

### Algorithm

1. Initialize positive wages $\{w_j^{(0)}\}$ and residential rents $\{q_i^{R,(0)}\}$.
2. Calculate $V_{ij}$, $\pi_{ij}$, and $N_{ij}$.
3. Aggregate $N_i=\sum_jN_{ij}$ and $L_j=\sum_iN_{ij}$.
4. Update residential rents:

   $$
   q_i^{R,\mathrm{new}}
   =
   \frac{(1-\alpha)\sum_jN_{ij}w_j}
   {\bar H_i^R}.
   $$

5. Update wages:

   $$
   w_j^{\mathrm{new}}
   =
   \gamma\bar A_j
   \left(
   \frac{\bar H_j^F}{L_j}
   \right)^{1-\gamma}.
   $$

6. Apply damping in logs:

   $$
   \log w_j^{(s+1)}
   =
   (1-\lambda)\log w_j^{(s)}
   +
   \lambda\log w_j^{\mathrm{new}},
   $$

   and similarly for $q_i^R$, with an initial damping factor such as
   $\lambda\in[0.05,0.25]$.

7. Repeat until the maximum proportional change in wages, rents, population, and employment falls below a stated tolerance.

The code should first be tested on simulated data generated by the model, where the true equilibrium is known.

---

## 4. Spatial units and sample definition

### Recommended spatial unit

Use census tracts or tract-sized regular grid cells.

- Census blocks create an unnecessarily large OD matrix and network.
- Counties are too coarse for zoning and transit access.
- Census tracts offer a practical compromise between spatial detail and computational cost.

### Recommended initial geography

Begin with one metropolitan area, such as Dallas–Fort Worth, before building a multi-city sample.

### Closed-city sample

The theoretical model fixes total population. The empirical sample should therefore retain workers whose residence and workplace are both inside the modeled metropolitan area.

External commuters can initially be handled by:

1. dropping flows with an endpoint outside the metropolitan area and redefining $N$ accordingly; or
2. adding an outside residence and an outside workplace as explicit outside options.

The second approach is ultimately preferable, but the first is simpler for the initial code test.

## 6. Parameters

### 6.1 Housing expenditure share

The model implies

$$
1-\alpha
=
\frac{\text{housing expenditure}}
{\text{labor income}}.
$$

This can be calibrated using ACS gross rent and household income for renter households. A reasonable preliminary range is

$$
1-\alpha\in[0.25,0.35],
\qquad
\alpha\in[0.65,0.75].
$$

The empirical measure must be consistent about whether utilities and other housing-related costs are included.

### 6.2 Labor share

The production function implies

$$
\gamma
=
\frac{w_jL_j}{Y_j}.
$$

Calibrate $\gamma$ using the labor share of value added for the industries represented in the metropolitan sample. A preliminary urban-economy value might fall between $0.65$ and $0.80$, but the preferred value should come from BEA industry accounts.

### 6.3 Location-choice elasticity

The OD choice equation can be expressed as

$$
\log N_{ij}
=
\delta_i^R+\delta_j^W
-\epsilon\log\tau_{ij}
+\text{error}_{ij},
$$

where residence and workplace fixed effects absorb rents, wages, amenities, $T_i$, and $E_j$.

Estimate this relationship using Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML):

$$
N_{ij}
=
\exp\left(
\delta_i^R+\delta_j^W-\epsilon\log\tau_{ij}
\right)
+\text{error}_{ij}.
$$

PPML accommodates zero flows and avoids log-linearization bias.

In the initial calibration, $\epsilon$ can instead be taken from the literature. A later version should use plausibly exogenous changes in transportation costs to identify it.

### 6.4 Travel-time conversion

If

$$
\tau_{ij}
=
\exp\left(\kappa\,\mathrm{time}_{ij}\right),
$$

then

$$
N_{ij}
\propto
\exp\left(
-\epsilon\kappa\,\mathrm{time}_{ij}
\right).
$$

An OD gravity regression identifies the product $\epsilon\kappa$, not $\epsilon$ and $\kappa$ separately.

Separating them requires at least one of:

- an externally calibrated value of time for $\kappa$;
- an externally estimated $\epsilon$; or
- additional identifying variation in wages, rents, or travel costs.

This identification issue should be stated explicitly.

## 7. Structural inversion

### 7.1 Productivity

Given $w_j$, $L_j$, $\bar H_j^F$, and $\gamma$, productivity is recovered from

$$
\boxed{
\bar A_j
=
\frac{w_j}{\gamma}
\left(
\frac{L_j}{\bar H_j^F}
\right)^{1-\gamma}
}.
$$

The productivity residual will include unmodeled industry composition, human capital, capital intensity, and workplace-specific productivity.

A possible normalization is

$$
\sum_j\frac{L_j}{N}\log\bar A_j=0.
$$

### 7.2 Residential and workplace attractiveness

The flow equation can be decomposed as

$$
N_{ij}
\propto
\underbrace{
T_i\bar B_i^\epsilon
(q_i^R)^{-\epsilon(1-\alpha)}
}_{\text{residence component}}
\underbrace{
E_jw_j^\epsilon
}_{\text{workplace component}}
\tau_{ij}^{-\epsilon}.
$$

This exposes an identification problem:

$$
T_i\bar B_i^\epsilon
$$

is identified as a composite, but $T_i$ and $\bar B_i$ are not separately identified from one cross section of flows.

For the benchmark, set

$$
T_i=1
$$

and interpret the recovered $\bar B_i$ as a composite residential fundamental.

After estimating origin and destination fixed effects, recover amenities from

$$
\log\bar B_i
=
\frac{\delta_i^R}{\epsilon}
+(1-\alpha)\log q_i^R
+\text{normalization},
$$

and workplace attractiveness from

$$
\log E_j
=
\delta_j^W-\epsilon\log w_j
+\text{normalization}.
$$

Possible normalizations are

$$
\sum_i\frac{N_i}{N}\log\bar B_i=0,
\qquad
\sum_j\frac{L_j}{N}\log E_j=0.
$$

### 7.3 Iterative proportional fitting

An alternative is to recover residence and workplace balancing factors using iterative proportional fitting.

Given $\epsilon$ and $\tau_{ij}$, select origin and destination factors so that predicted flows exactly match:

$$
\sum_j\widehat N_{ij}=N_i^{\mathrm{data}},
\qquad
\sum_i\widehat N_{ij}=L_j^{\mathrm{data}}.
$$

This procedure exactly targets residential and employment margins while allowing travel costs and $\epsilon$ to determine the bilateral allocation within those margins.

---

## 8. Quantities versus prices

Residential market clearing implies

$$
q_i^R\bar H_i^R
=
(1-\alpha)\sum_jN_{ij}w_j.
$$

If rents, wages, and flows are observed, the implied effective capacity is

$$
\boxed{
\bar H_i^R
=
\frac{(1-\alpha)\sum_jN_{ij}w_j}
{q_i^R}
}.
$$

The calibration must choose between:

1. measuring $\bar H_i^R$ and allowing the model to predict $q_i^R$; or
2. measuring $q_i^R$ and inverting effective $\bar H_i^R$.

Both capacity and rent cannot be freely calibrated to match the same market-clearing equation.

For the first benchmark, the second option is easier and can reproduce baseline rents exactly. The resulting object should be described as **effective residential capacity**, rather than literal physical floor space.

Similarly, if commercial rents are observed,

$$
\boxed{
\bar H_j^F
=
\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}
\frac{w_jL_j}{q_j^F}
}.
$$

The same measured-quantity-versus-inverted-price choice must be made for the commercial market.

---

## 9. Recommended first calibration

The initial implementation should use:

- **Geography:** one metropolitan area;
- **Spatial unit:** census tract;
- **Population:** workers living and working inside the metropolitan area;
- **OD flows:** LODES primary jobs;
- **Travel cost:** fixed peak-period automobile travel time;
- **Housing quantity:** effective standardized housing units;
- **Commercial capacity:** measured commercial floor area where available;
- **Housing share:** ACS rent-to-income information;
- **Labor share:** external calibration from BEA accounts;
- **Travel sensitivity:** estimate $\epsilon\kappa$ using PPML;
- **Choice dispersion:** calibrate $\epsilon$ externally at first;
- **Productivity:** invert $\bar A_j$;
- **Residential amenities:** set $T_i=1$ and invert $\bar B_i$;
- **Workplace attractiveness:** invert $E_j$;
- **Agglomeration and congestion:** set $\zeta=a=b=0$;
- **Policies:** hold zoning capacities and transportation costs fixed.

The benchmark should reproduce:

1. total modeled population;
2. residential population by tract;
3. employment by tract;
4. bilateral commuting flows;
5. residential rents or residential capacity, depending on the calibration choice; and
6. workplace wages or productivity, depending on the calibration choice.

---

## 10. Validation

Convergence is necessary but not sufficient. The benchmark should be evaluated using:

- correlation and error between predicted and observed $N_i$;
- correlation and error between predicted and observed $L_j$;
- OD-flow fit, including zero and low-flow pairs;
- average and distribution of commute times;
- rent fit;
- wage fit;
- implied housing expenditure shares;
- implied commercial rent-to-wage-bill ratios;
- fit by distance bin;
- fit by municipality; and
- out-of-sample fit after holding out selected OD pairs, tracts, or years.

If unrestricted location fixed effects are inverted, the targeted margins will fit mechanically. Meaningful validation must therefore focus on untargeted outcomes, such as:

- bilateral flows not used in the inversion;
- commute-time distributions;
- rents or wages not directly targeted;
- another year of data; or
- responses to an observed transportation change.

---

## 11. Sequence for extending the model

After the exogenous-characteristics equilibrium has been coded, calibrated, and validated, add the remaining mechanisms one at a time:

1. multimodal route choice and $\theta$;
2. edge-level traffic;
3. endogenous congestion;
4. congestion parameters $\rho$ and $\beta$;
5. productivity agglomeration $\zeta$;
6. residential congestion parameters $a$ and $b$;
7. endogenous municipal zoning;
8. transportation-authority investment;
9. metropolitan zoning coordination; and
10. the joint social-planner counterfactual.

At every stage, the extended model should reproduce the preceding model when the new mechanism is switched off.
