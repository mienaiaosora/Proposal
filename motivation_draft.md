# Motivation Draft

## The Problem: Stagnant Transit in U.S. Cities

U.S. cities have historically underinvested in public transit relative to comparable developed nations. Recent work documents that while transit networks are expanding globally, the United States has stagnated since the 1980s. By 2023, the total metro length in the U.S. was surpassed by India and approached by the EU, despite larger population and GDP. Even accounting for light rail and streetcar networks, transit construction has declined since the 1970s and struggles to keep pace with population growth. Understanding why policymakers choose not to invest in transit is non-trivial and points to the endogenous nature of infrastructure decisions.

## Transit Returns and Housing Supply: A Literature Gap

Transportation infrastructure reduces spatial friction and can boost economic development by connecting workers to opportunities, but its effectiveness depends critically on urban form. Rail-based transit, in particular, can increase station-adjacent development, promote density, and encourage mixed-use activity. Standard approaches to evaluating transit benefits measure travel time savings and commuting benefits.

However, recent work suggests transit returns may be limited by restrictive housing supply. Restrictive zoning can prevent transit access from translating into higher density and development around stations. Recent findings show limited economic returns from transit investments in cities with restrictive zoning and polycentric employment structures. **This suggests that transit and zoning are not independent---the viability of transit depends on the regulatory environment for housing density.**

## The Endogeneity of Zoning

Zoning is itself an endogenous policy choice, typically motivated by incumbent residents' desire to protect property values and neighborhood quality. Restrictive zoning limits housing supply in desirable locations, which locally benefits existing residents but creates city-wide costs: households are priced out of transit-accessible areas and pushed toward distant, car-dependent locations. This increases job-housing separation and contributes to urban sprawl.

## A Vicious Cycle: Transit, Zoning, and Sprawl

The interaction between zoning and transportation creates a self-reinforcing equilibrium:

- Restrictive zoning near transit stations reduces station-area density, lowering transit ridership and returns on transit investment.
- With limited transit, commuting by car becomes the dominant mode, and residents can afford to live far from jobs.
- Decentralized settlement patterns increase demand for low-density, car-oriented housing, which residents protect through restrictive zoning.
- With dispersed development, transit becomes even less viable, and policymakers choose road expansion instead.
- Better roads further facilitate sprawl, completing the cycle.

This cycle produces a car-dependent urban form that is rational at the household level but inefficient at the metropolitan level.

## Why This Matters: Sprawl's Broader Costs

The equilibrium described above---low-density, car-dependent development---imposes costs that individual households do not internalize:

- **Fiscal costs**: Low-density sprawl requires more infrastructure (roads, utilities, emergency services) per capita, raising the fiscal burden on cities.
- **Congestion and pollution**: Car-dependent commuting increases congestion, emissions, and time costs that are not reflected in household location choices.
- **Lost agglomeration benefits**: Dispersed employment patterns reduce matching efficiency in labor markets and limit the productivity gains from urban density.
- **Inequality**: Restrictive zoning in desirable areas prices out lower-income households, perpetuating residential segregation and limiting economic mobility.

## Research Question

This proposal investigates **how the interaction between zoning regulation and transportation infrastructure decisions contributes to inefficient urban sprawl in U.S. metropolitan areas**. Specifically, we ask: to what extent do restrictive zoning policies and underinvestment in transit reinforce each other, and what are the welfare consequences of this equilibrium?
