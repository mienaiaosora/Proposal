# How Zoning & Transportation Decisions Are Made in the U.S.
*Dallas as a case study, compared against national variation*

---

## 1. Zoning

### Dallas Model: City-Wide Authority, Neighborhood Input (Not a Vote)

- **Level of decision:** City-wide, not neighborhood-by-neighborhood.
- **Process:**
  1. **City Plan Commission (CPC)** — 15 members, one appointed per City Council district — reviews zoning cases and the comprehensive plan, holds public hearings, and makes *recommendations only*.
  2. **City Council** holds the final, binding vote.
- **Public input mechanisms:** Mailed notices to nearby property owners, two separate public hearings (Commission, then Council) — but no binding neighborhood vote.
- **Example:** *Forward Dallas* (2024) — the first major citywide zoning overhaul in 18 years, allowing multiplexes in areas currently zoned single-family. Passed Council 11–4 despite vocal neighborhood opposition.

### Other places

| Model | Where Used | Who Decides | Binding on Residents? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Centralized council** | Dallas & most big Sun Belt/Southern cities | Elected Council, advised by appointed Commission | Yes — only via elected representatives |
| **Neighborhood advisory boards** | New York City (59 community boards) | Council still decides; boards review/recommend. Board members are **appointed** (half by Borough President, half nominated by City Council member) — not elected by residents | No — advisory only |
| **Open Town Meeting** | ~300 of Massachusetts's 351 towns (mandatory under 6,000 residents); common across New England | **Every registered voter**, directly, at an in-person meeting | **Yes — true direct democracy**, including zoning bylaw changes |
| **Representative Town Meeting** | Larger New England towns (e.g., Brookline, since 1915) | Elected Town Meeting Members vote; public may attend and speak | Yes, but via elected representatives |

**Key driver of the difference:** population size and regional tradition, not federal law. New England's town meeting tradition dates to the colonial era; the 6,000-resident threshold exists because direct voting becomes impractical at larger scale — which is exactly why Dallas (1.3M+ residents) was never going to use this model.

## 2. Transportation

Transportation governance splits into three distinct layers, each with its own pattern: **planning**, **transit operations**, and **funding**.

### A. Regional Planning (MPO Layer) — Consistently Representative Nationwide

- Every U.S. metro area is federally required to have a **Metropolitan Planning Organization (MPO)**.
- In Dallas–Fort Worth: the **North Central Texas Council of Governments (NCTCOG)** staffs the MPO; the **Regional Transportation Council (RTC)** — 44 members, local elected/appointed officials plus transportation-provider representatives — is the actual policymaking body.
- This layer is representative-of-representatives almost everywhere in the U.S. **No direct public vote occurs at the MPO/planning level in any major metro.**

### B. Transit Agency Boards — Real National Variation

| Governance Model | Example | How Board Members Get There |
|---|---|---|
| **All locally appointed** | **DART** (Dallas) | 15 members appointed by service-area city councils based on population; Dallas alone appoints 8 of 15 |
| **Multi-jurisdiction appointed** | **WMATA** (DC Metro) | 8 voting + 8 alternate directors; Maryland, DC, Virginia, and the federal government each appoint two voting + two alternate |
| **Directly elected by the public** | **BART**, AC Transit (Bay Area) | Board members are directly elected by voters — a genuine exception to the appointed-board norm |
| **No independent board** | Maryland Transit Administration | Folded into the state DOT; overseen by a governor-appointed secretary |
| **County-based single operator** | LA Metro, King County Metro (Seattle), Miami-Dade Transit | One agency serves the most urbanized county; other agencies cover commuter rail outside it |

### C. Funding — Where Direct Voting Is Actually Common

Unlike planning and (mostly) board governance, **funding decisions frequently go to direct ballot measures** nationwide:

- Voters approved **$24.9 billion** in transit ballot measures in the November 2024 election alone — an **86.7% win rate** (51 of 61 measures passed nationwide in 2024).
- Typical mechanism: sales tax or property tax measures that fund transit operations directly (e.g., Richland County, SC, renewed a one-cent transportation sales tax with over 60% approval).
- **Active 2026 example:** Five Bay Area counties will vote in November 2026 on a sales tax (0.5%, up to 1% in San Francisco) to fund BART, Muni, Caltrain, and AC Transit — a citizen-led initiative that gathered over 300,000 signatures to qualify.

### Dallas-Specific Direct-Vote Mechanism: DART Withdrawal Elections

- Texas law allows **DART member cities to hold a withdrawal election every six years**, letting residents vote directly on whether to leave the transit agency.
- As of 2026, several member cities have called these elections — driven partly by frustration that Dallas (appointing 8 of 15 board seats) dominates a board that's supposed to serve 13 cities regionally.
- This is a rare instance of **direct public voting overriding a representative governance structure** in transportation.

## 3. Putting It Together: The Same Underlying Pattern

| | Default Model | Direct-Vote Exception |
|---|---|---|
| **Zoning** | City Council decides (Dallas-style); advisory neighborhood boards in some cities (NYC) | New England Open Town Meeting — voters directly approve zoning bylaws |
| **Transportation — planning** | MPO/RTC, appointed/elected officials | None — no direct vote anywhere in the U.S. |
| **Transportation — board governance** | Mostly appointed (DART, WMATA); state-DOT-run in some states | BART/AC Transit — directly elected boards |
| **Transportation — funding** | Council/board approves budget | **Ballot measures (sales/property tax) — the norm nationwide**, plus DART-style withdrawal elections |

### Key Takeaway

There is no single national system for either zoning or transportation governance — both are **state and local design choices**, shaped primarily by population size and regional tradition rather than federal law.

- For **day-to-day planning decisions** (zoning classifications, road/transit project selection), **representative bodies** make the call almost everywhere — Dallas's model (elected council + appointed commission/board) is the most common pattern in the U.S.
- For **big-ticket structural and funding questions** (should we tax ourselves for a rail line; should our city stay in a regional transit agency; should this town allow multifamily zoning), **direct voter approval is common and sometimes the norm** — exemplified by New England's zoning town meetings, nationwide transit funding ballot measures, and Dallas's own 2026 DART withdrawal elections.
