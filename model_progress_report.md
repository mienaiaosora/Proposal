# Model Progress Report

**Date:** July 28, 2026  
**Main drafts reviewed:** `model_rederived.tex` and `model_bordeu_objective_FAR.tex`

## Executive summary

The project asks how fragmented municipal zoning changes the spatial allocation of residents and jobs and, through those changes, alters the metropolitan transportation authority's investment priorities. The central comparison is between:

1. a decentralized equilibrium in which municipalities choose zoning locally and a metropolitan authority allocates a fixed transportation budget; and
2. an integrated metropolitan planner that chooses zoning and transportation investment jointly.

`model_rederived.tex` establishes the basic mechanism with a fixed amount of buildable floor space at each location. Municipalities can change only its composition between residential and commercial uses. The draft derives the household and firm equilibrium, recursive network choice, municipal and transportation problems, the metropolitan aggregation result, and the welfare wedge generated by local competition for residents and workers.

`model_bordeu_objective_FAR.tex` makes an important extension. Physical land is now primitive, and municipalities choose both total permitted development intensity (FAR) and the residential–commercial composition of that capacity. This separates two conceptually different distortions:

- an **intensity distortion**, in which a municipality permits too much or too little total floor space; and
- a **composition distortion**, in which it assigns the permitted capacity inefficiently between housing and employment.

This is real progress. However, the FAR extension is not yet a closed economic model. In particular, there is no construction or development cost that disciplines the choice of FAR, and the proposed conversion-wedge-to-share mapping holds commercial rent fixed even though commercial rent is endogenous in the model. Consequently, the current draft can characterize desired first-order conditions, but it cannot yet establish that a finite FAR equilibrium exists or that the regulatory wedge uniquely implements the stated residential share in general equilibrium.

## What the project is trying to solve

The research problem has four connected parts.

### 1. Municipal zoning competition

Each municipality values metropolitan household welfare but also places political weight on the residents and workers attached to its jurisdiction. A local zoning change therefore affects both:

- common metropolitan welfare; and
- the municipality's own weighted political base.

This gives the municipality an incentive to manipulate the location of residents and employment even when doing so lowers metropolitan welfare.

### 2. Cross-jurisdictional externalities

A municipality internalizes its own objective but not the effects of its zoning decision on other jurisdictions. Zoning changes rents, wages, amenities, employment, commuting flows, route use, and congestion throughout the network. The draft's central theoretical result is that, at a decentralized municipal optimum, the remaining metropolitan welfare gradient equals the cross-jurisdictional effect ignored by the municipality.

### 3. Zoning and transportation interaction

Zoning changes where people live and work. This changes origin–destination flows, traffic on individual network edges, and the marginal welfare value of transportation investment. Thus, fragmented zoning can cause a metropolitan transportation authority to allocate a fixed budget differently from an integrated planner, even if the transportation authority behaves optimally conditional on the zoning it observes.

### 4. The scale of government

The model compares municipal zoning decisions with metropolitan zoning coordination. Because municipal resident and worker masses add up to fixed metropolitan totals, the attraction terms cancel when municipal objectives are aggregated. A metropolitan zoning authority therefore maximizes the same common utility index as the transportation authority. This isolates fragmentation—not a different household utility concept—as the source of the zoning distortion.

## Progress in `model_rederived.tex`

The rederived model provides a coherent baseline.

### Spatial equilibrium

The draft integrates:

- endogenous residence–workplace choice;
- residential rent clearing;
- firm labor demand and commercial floor-space demand;
- productivity agglomeration;
- local congestion or amenity effects;
- recursive network paths rather than a finite, exogenously enumerated route set;
- endogenous edge traffic and congestion; and
- edge-level transportation investment.

This is a substantial improvement over a reduced-form model in which accessibility or travel cost is imposed externally.

### Government problems

The decentralized equilibrium clearly separates:

- municipalities choosing local residential versus commercial capacity; and
- a metropolitan transportation authority allocating investment across edges.

The authorities move simultaneously and take the other policy block as given. The equilibrium definition now states the household, firm, network, market-clearing, municipal, and transportation conditions in one place.

### Welfare comparison

The draft derives three useful results:

1. **Planner dominance:** the integrated planner weakly improves household welfare because the decentralized allocation is feasible for the planner.
2. **Municipal zoning wedge:** the welfare gradient at the decentralized equilibrium is proportional to the municipality's incentive to attract weighted resident–worker mass.
3. **Cross-jurisdictional interpretation:** the metropolitan welfare gradient equals the effect on other municipal objectives that the choosing municipality ignores.

The first-order welfare-gap expression then connects the decentralized-to-planner zoning change to the local attraction terms. This gives the theory a potentially measurable empirical object.

### Transportation allocation

The transportation section develops an adjoint or multiplier representation of the marginal value of each edge. Conditional on zoning and a fixed budget, investment is allocated according to traffic, congestion technology, edge cost, and the edge's equilibrium market-access shadow value. This is the mechanism connecting zoning to infrastructure allocation.

## Progress in `model_bordeu_objective_FAR.tex`

The newer draft resolves an important limitation of the baseline: fixed total floor-space capacity.

### Physical land and FAR

The model now starts from fixed physical land, \(\bar L_i\), and defines total permitted capacity as

\[
\bar H_i=z_i\bar L_i.
\]

Residential and commercial capacities are

\[
\bar H_i^R=s_i z_i\bar L_i,
\qquad
\bar H_i^F=(1-s_i)z_i\bar L_i.
\]

This makes the economic roles of the two zoning margins transparent:

- \(z_i\) controls total intensity;
- \(s_i\) controls use composition.

The old model is nested as the special case in which \(z_i\) is fixed.

### Two-channel derivative decomposition

The chain-rule decomposition is one of the strongest additions. A change in FAR expands residential and commercial capacity together in their existing shares, whereas a change in composition increases one use at the expense of the other. For any equilibrium outcome \(Y\), the draft separates:

- the density-weighted average of the residential and commercial capacity effects; and
- the difference between those two effects.

This gives a clean interpretation of the two municipal first-order conditions.

### Extension of the welfare wedge

The municipal distortion and cross-jurisdictional externality are extended to both margins. The newer draft therefore shows that a municipality could choose the correct composition but the wrong total intensity, or vice versa. That distinction was impossible in the baseline model.

### Updated planner comparison

The metropolitan authority and integrated planner now choose both intensity and composition. The aggregation argument continues to work because changes in zoning redistribute resident and worker mass across municipalities without changing metropolitan totals.

## Main unresolved technical problems

### 1. The FAR choice is not disciplined by a development technology

The model assumes that all permitted floor-space capacity is built and fully utilized, but it contains neither construction costs nor a developer's supply problem. Increasing \(z_i\) therefore creates additional residential and commercial floor space mechanically.

This creates two problems:

- there is no demonstrated reason for a municipality or planner to choose a finite interior \(z_i\); and
- a legal FAR cap is treated as actual constructed floor space, even when market demand would not support full build-out.

The model needs either:

- a convex cost of permitting or supplying intensity;
- an explicit developer problem with land and construction inputs;
- an upper bound on feasible FAR treated as a technological constraint; or
- a distinction between permitted capacity and actual floor space, with complementary slackness.

Until this is added, the FAR first-order condition is a formal derivative, not a complete equilibrium characterization.

### 2. The regulatory wedge does not yet generate a valid general-equilibrium share map

The draft derives

\[
\frac{ds_i}{dt_i}=\frac{s_i}{t_i}
\]

by treating commercial rent \(q_i^F\) as locally fixed. But elsewhere \(q_i^F\) depends on commercial capacity, employment, wages, and agglomeration. Changing \(t_i\) changes \(s_i\), which changes \(\bar H_i^F\), so \(q_i^F\) generally changes as well.

Therefore:

- the unit-elastic mapping is not a full general-equilibrium identity;
- the mapping need not remain one-to-one globally;
- integrating the local formula gives \(s_i=C_it_i\), which does not automatically remain in \([0,1]\); and
- the assertion that choosing \(t_i\) is exactly equivalent to choosing any \(s_i\) is currently too strong.

There is also an algebraic inconsistency in the final welfare-gap discussion. If the maintained local derivative is \(ds_i/dt_i=s_i/t_i\), then the first-order conversion is

\[
s_i^{SP}-s_i^{DE}
\approx
\frac{s_i^{DE}}{t_i^{DE}}
\left(t_i^{SP}-t_i^{DE}\right),
\]

not \((z_i\bar L_i/t_i)(t_i^{SP}-t_i^{DE})\). The latter has the units of floor space and would apply to a derivative of \(\bar H_i^R\), not to the dimensionless share \(s_i\).

A correct implementation equation must differentiate both rents:

\[
t_i q_i^R(s_i,z_i,x)=q_i^F(s_i,z_i,x),
\]

where \(x\) contains the full equilibrium response. Alternatively, the model can let the municipality choose \(s_i\) directly and interpret \(t_i=q_i^F/q_i^R\) only as the decentralizing wedge observed after equilibrium.

### 3. The wedge is simultaneously described as a real resource cost and an implementing price wedge

If \(t_i\) destroys resources, the lost resources should enter feasibility and welfare. If it is a tax or regulatory charge, the recipient or revenue treatment should be specified. At present it is excluded from household income and municipal welfare, but no explicit resource constraint records the loss.

The model must choose one interpretation:

- a real conversion cost;
- a tax with revenue rebated or spent;
- an ownership restriction;
- or a reduced-form implementation wedge with no separate welfare accounting.

### 4. The municipal objective remains economically unusual

Municipal welfare is \(W_g=M_g\bar U\), where all households share the same ex ante metropolitan utility index and \(M_g\) is a weighted count of local residents and workers. This is analytically useful, but it is not yet derived from voting, tax revenue, landowner welfare, incumbent residents, or a fiscal objective.

The model can use this objective, but the interpretation must be explicit: it is a reduced-form political-attraction objective. The weights \(\omega_R\) and \(\omega_F\) will otherwise be difficult to identify empirically.

### 5. Existence, uniqueness, and concavity are not established

The main results use interior first-order conditions and sometimes invoke joint concavity. The current drafts do not establish:

- existence of the spatial equilibrium with agglomeration and congestion;
- uniqueness of the recursive-path and spatial fixed point;
- existence of municipal best responses;
- existence or uniqueness of the municipal Nash equilibrium; or
- concavity of the reduced-form policy problem.

The planner-dominance result does not require all of these conditions, but claims equating coordinated authorities with the integrated planner require more than equality of first-order conditions.

### 6. Boundary solutions are economically likely

The derivations emphasize interior \(s_i\), \(z_i\), and edge investment. In practice:

- a location may specialize in residential or commercial use;
- a FAR constraint may be slack;
- some transportation edges may receive zero investment; and
- the wedge-to-share arbitrage condition applies only when both uses are active.

The final model needs Kuhn–Tucker conditions or a clear maintained assumption restricting attention to an interior equilibrium.

### 7. Transportation mode is still not structural

Roads and transit are represented by one generic edge technology. The model cannot currently distinguish:

- road congestion from transit crowding;
- service frequency from physical capacity;
- fixed transit operating costs;
- fares and subsidies;
- mode choice; or
- transfers between modes.

It can study allocation across abstract network links, but it cannot yet make road-versus-transit policy claims.

### 8. The total transportation budget is fixed

The transportation authority reallocates a given \(K\). This identifies spatial misallocation across edges, but not aggregate underinvestment or overinvestment. Because the budget is fixed, the model cannot show that fragmented zoning reduces the total amount invested.

An endogenous budget requires an investment cost or financing problem, such as

\[
\max_{\mathbf I\geq0}\ \bar U(\mathbf I,\mathbf z)-C(\mathbf I),
\]

possibly with taxes, intergovernmental transfers, or fare revenue.

### 9. Recursive edge use needs careful interpretation

The recursive-path construction sums over walks, including walks that may revisit an edge. The matrix expression naturally gives expected edge traversals conditional on an OD pair, not necessarily the probability of using an edge at least once. The terminology and traffic equation should use “expected traversal count” unless repeated-edge walks are ruled out.

The spectral-radius condition also needs an economic or parametric justification to ensure that the infinite walk sum is finite.

## What the current model can claim

At this stage, the model can support the following claims:

- Local zoning decisions affect the entire metropolitan equilibrium, not only outcomes inside the choosing jurisdiction.
- Municipal competition for residents and workers creates a wedge between local and metropolitan zoning conditions.
- A metropolitan zoning authority eliminates the local attraction terms under the stated objective and fixed-total-population assumptions.
- Conditional on a fixed transportation budget, zoning affects optimal investment shares by changing OD flows, edge traffic, congestion, and market-access values.
- Separating FAR and land-use composition reveals two distinct potential zoning distortions.

## What the current model cannot yet claim

The current drafts cannot yet establish:

- whether decentralized municipalities choose FAR that is systematically too high or too low;
- whether restrictive zoning causes aggregate transportation underinvestment;
- whether a finite, unique FAR equilibrium exists;
- that the proposed regulatory wedge implements an arbitrary residential share in full general equilibrium;
- how much welfare is lost without calibration or estimation;
- whether roads or transit receive too much investment relative to one another;
- causal empirical effects of zoning using the theoretical model alone;
- a globally valid planner–decentralized welfare-gap approximation; or
- that coordinated zoning and transportation authorities always attain the global integrated-planner optimum without concavity.

## Quantification and data limitations

The newly added NLLUS data are useful for the zoning side of the project. They provide repeated information on zoning ordinances, maximum density, growth controls, impact fees, adequate-public-facilities requirements, inclusionary zoning, and related regulations, with Census housing and demographic outcomes.

However, NLLUS does not directly measure:

- parcel-level permitted FAR for all locations;
- residential and commercial floor-space shares;
- commercial rents and floor space;
- municipal political weights;
- commuting OD flows;
- network edge traffic;
- travel time and congestion technology;
- edge-level capital stocks or investment costs; or
- total municipal or metropolitan transportation investment.

The model therefore cannot be taken directly to the NLLUS panel without additional data and measurement assumptions. LODES can provide workplace and commuting-flow information; transit and road network sources can provide edges and service; building permits, assessor data, zoning maps, or parcel data are needed to measure actual and permitted intensity.

## Recommended next steps

The highest-priority theoretical choice is whether FAR is:

1. a binding legal cap on developer-chosen construction; or
2. floor space directly supplied by the municipality.

The first interpretation is more realistic and connects naturally to the available housing-supply evidence. It suggests the following sequence:

1. Add a developer problem that determines actual residential and commercial floor space subject to the FAR cap.
2. Permit slack caps and write the associated complementary-slackness conditions.
3. Let municipalities choose \(z_i\) and either choose \(s_i\) directly or derive the full general-equilibrium wedge implementation without holding \(q_i^F\) fixed.
4. Re-derive the municipal and planner conditions using actual, rather than automatically fully utilized, floor space.
5. Decide whether the conversion wedge is a real resource cost, a tax, or only an implementation device.
6. Retain the generic network and fixed budget for the first quantitative version; treat modal technology and endogenous aggregate investment as later extensions.
7. Build a minimal numerical model before expanding the theory further. A small network can reveal whether equilibria exist, whether FAR binds, and which comparative statics are actually robust.

## Bottom line

`model_rederived.tex` supplies a strong baseline theory of fragmented zoning, spatial equilibrium, and conditional transportation allocation. `model_bordeu_objective_FAR.tex` makes the right conceptual move by separating total development intensity from residential–commercial composition. The main remaining task is not another algebraic extension. It is to close the supply and implementation side of the FAR model so that permitted intensity, actual construction, rents, and the regulatory wedge are jointly consistent.
